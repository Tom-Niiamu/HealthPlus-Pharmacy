Antibiotics: When You Need Them and When You Don't

May 2026  ·  4 min read

Pharmacy May 2026 4 min read
Abena GyasiRegistered Pharmacist · HealthPlus Pharmacy

Walk into almost any pharmacy in Accra and you can buy amoxicillin over the counter. Many Ghanaians treat this as a first line of defence whenever they feel unwell. The problem is that antibiotics only work on bacterial infections — and the majority of illnesses that make people reach for them are caused by viruses, which antibiotics cannot touch.

What Antibiotics Actually Do

Antibiotics are medicines designed to kill or stop the growth of bacteria. They have transformed medicine — making surgery safer, curing tuberculosis, saving lives from sepsis. But they have no effect whatsoever on viruses like the flu, the common cold, COVID-19, or most sore throats and ear infections.

70%

of antibiotic prescriptions globally are for respiratory infections — of which the majority are viral and do not require antibiotics.

2050

is when antibiotic-resistant infections are projected to kill more people than cancer if overuse continues at current rates.

Conditions That DO Require Antibiotics

  • Confirmed bacterial pneumonia — diagnosed by a doctor with a chest X-ray or clinical assessment
  • Urinary tract infections (UTIs) — burning on urination, frequent urge, confirmed by urine dipstick
  • Strep throat — severe sore throat with high fever and white patches, confirmed by throat swab
  • Skin infections — spreading redness, warmth, swelling around a wound
  • Sexually transmitted infections — chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis (bacterial STIs)
  • Typhoid fever — confirmed by blood culture or Widal test
Key principle

A bacterial infection needs to be confirmed — either by a doctor's clinical assessment or a lab test — before antibiotics are the right choice. "I feel sick" is not sufficient reason to start a course.

Conditions That Do NOT Require Antibiotics

  • Common cold
  • Flu (influenza)
  • Most sore throats
  • Most ear infections in children (the majority resolve on their own)
  • Most cases of diarrhoea (usually viral or from contaminated food — ORS and zinc are the evidence-based treatment)
  • Chest infections that start alongside a cold

The Right Way to Use Antibiotics When Prescribed

1

Complete the full course

Even if you feel better after 2–3 days, finish every tablet. Stopping early leaves resistant bacteria alive.

2

Take at the right times

Spacing doses correctly maintains the antibiotic level in your blood. Set phone reminders if needed.

3

Never share or reuse

Do not give your prescription to a family member, and do not save leftover tablets for "next time."

4

Tell your pharmacist about allergies

Penicillin allergy is the most common drug allergy. Always mention it before being dispensed amoxicillin.

Antibiotic resistance is a real threat in Ghana

Drug-resistant typhoid, drug-resistant TB and resistant UTI bacteria are all increasing in Accra. Every unnecessary antibiotic course contributes to this problem — and makes infections harder to treat for everyone.
