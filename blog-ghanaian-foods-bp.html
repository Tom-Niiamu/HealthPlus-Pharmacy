The Best Ghanaian Foods for Managing High Blood Pressure

April 2026  ·  5 min read

Nutrition April 2026 5 min read
Maame Serwah AsanteRegistered Dietitian · HealthPlus Pharmacy

Hypertension — high blood pressure — affects an estimated 30% of Ghanaian adults, and the number is rising. The standard medical advice of "cut the salt and eat more vegetables" is technically correct but practically useless without knowing what that means for someone whose daily meals are built around fufu, kelewele, waakye and palm nut soup.

The good news: Ghanaian cuisine contains several genuinely heart-healthy ingredients. The challenge is preparation — and a few habits that, with small adjustments, can dramatically reduce your cardiovascular risk while still eating the food you love.

Why Diet Matters for Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is affected by sodium (raises it), potassium (lowers it), magnesium, dietary fibre, and overall calorie intake. The DASH diet — the most evidence-based dietary approach for hypertension — emphasises fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins and low-fat dairy. Most of these principles translate directly into Ghanaian cooking once you know where to look.

Foods to Eat More Of

Garden Eggs (African Eggplant)

One of the best-kept secrets in Ghanaian nutrition. Garden eggs are rich in potassium, low in sodium, and contain nasunin — an antioxidant that protects blood vessel walls. Garden egg stew made without excess salt is an excellent choice for hypertensive patients.

Kontomire (Cocoyam Leaves)

A powerhouse of potassium, calcium and magnesium — the three minerals most directly linked to healthy blood pressure. Kontomire stew, prepared without salted fish or at least with less of it, is one of the most cardiovascular-friendly staples in Ghanaian cuisine.

Tomatoes

Used in almost every Ghanaian sauce, tomatoes contain lycopene and potassium. Fresh tomatoes — as opposed to tomato paste, which is often high in added sodium — provide the most benefit. When making stews, using fresh blended tomatoes rather than commercial paste is a simple and effective swap.

Bananas and Plantain

Both are excellent sources of potassium. Unripe plantain — boiled or roasted rather than fried — has a lower glycaemic index than ripe plantain and provides sustained energy without a blood sugar spike. Kelewele (spiced fried ripe plantain) is delicious but the frying and added sugar make it an occasional treat rather than a daily food.

Beans (Cowpeas / Black-Eyed Beans)

Waakye's bean component is genuinely excellent for cardiovascular health — high in fibre, potassium, and magnesium. The challenge is how waakye is typically served: with large amounts of stew, spaghetti, fried plantain, and gari. The beans themselves are heart-healthy; the full plate needs portion awareness.

The salt problem

The biggest single dietary driver of hypertension in Ghana is salt — not just the salt added during cooking, but the hidden salt in Maggi cubes, fermented fish (momoni, koobi), salted herrings, and commercial condiments. Switching to low-sodium stock cubes and reducing fermented fish portions are the highest-impact changes most Ghanaians can make.

Simple Swaps That Make a Real Difference

1

Replace Maggi with low-sodium stock

A single Maggi cube contains around 900mg of sodium — nearly half the daily recommended limit for hypertensive patients.

2

Reduce koobi and momoni portions

These add enormous amounts of sodium. Using smaller quantities with more fresh tomatoes and onion provides the depth of flavour with less cardiovascular risk.

3

Choose boiled or roasted over fried

Boiled yam, boiled plantain, and roasted corn all retain far more nutrients and contain significantly fewer calories than their fried equivalents.

4

Add more vegetables to every meal

Even adding a handful of spinach or kontomire to a stew, or a side salad to rice, increases potassium intake meaningfully over time.
