10 Signs You Should See a Doctor This Week

June 2026  ·  5 min read

Prevention June 2026 5 min read
Dr. Ama BoatengGeneral Practitioner · HealthPlus Pharmacy

Most of us have learned to push through minor aches and ignore the small signals our bodies send us. But occasionally, a symptom that feels trivial is the early warning of something that — caught now — is straightforward to treat. Missed, it can become serious.

Here are ten signs that warrant a doctor's appointment this week, not "eventually."

1. Unexplained Weight Loss

Losing more than 5% of your body weight in six months without trying — no new diet, no new exercise — can signal thyroid problems, diabetes, depression, or in some cases cancer. It should always be investigated promptly.

What to tell your doctor

Track when the weight loss started, how much you have lost, and whether you have any other symptoms like fatigue, increased thirst, or changes in bowel habits.

2. Persistent Fatigue That Rest Doesn't Fix

Feeling tired after a rough week is normal. Feeling exhausted every single day — regardless of how much you sleep — is not. Persistent fatigue is one of the most common presentations of anaemia, thyroid disease, diabetes, depression, and sleep apnoea.

3. Chest Pain or Tightness

⚠️ When it is an emergency

If your chest pain is severe, spreading to your arm or jaw, or accompanied by sweating and shortness of breath — call 999 immediately. Do not drive yourself.

Mild or intermittent chest discomfort, pressure, or tightness — even if it comes and goes — should still be evaluated within days. It can represent acid reflux, musculoskeletal issues, or early cardiac disease.

4. A Cough That Has Lasted More Than 3 Weeks

Most coughs from colds and chest infections clear within two to three weeks. A cough that persists beyond that — especially with blood-stained mucus, weight loss, or night sweats — needs investigation to rule out TB, which remains prevalent in Ghana, as well as asthma and lung disease.

5. Unusual Lumps or Swellings

Any new lump — in the breast, neck, armpit, groin or anywhere else — that is hard, growing, or painless deserves prompt medical attention. Most lumps are benign, but some are not, and early evaluation is always better than waiting.

6. Changes in Bowel or Bladder Habits

Blood in your stool or urine, persistent diarrhoea lasting more than a week, or a change in your normal bowel pattern that you cannot explain with diet should be checked. These symptoms can indicate infections, inflammatory conditions, or early colorectal cancer.

7. Severe or Unusual Headaches

A headache that is the "worst of your life," comes on suddenly like a thunderclap, or wakes you from sleep is a red flag for meningitis, a bleed in the brain, or extreme high blood pressure. Even if it fades quickly — see a doctor the same day.

8. High Blood Pressure Readings at Home

If your home monitor consistently shows readings above 140/90 mmHg, book an appointment. Hypertension is called "the silent killer" because it causes no symptoms until it damages the heart, kidneys, or brain. It is also one of the most manageable conditions with the right treatment.

9. Vision or Speech Changes

⚠️ Stroke warning — FAST

Face drooping · Arm weakness · Speech slurring · Time to call 999. Stroke symptoms require emergency care, not a routine appointment.

Sudden blurring, double vision, or difficulty finding words — even briefly — are symptoms of TIA (mini-stroke). These are medical emergencies that predict a full stroke within 48 hours if untreated.

10. Mood Changes That Affect Your Daily Life

Depression, anxiety and other mental health conditions are medical illnesses — not character flaws. If your mood, sleep, motivation or ability to function has changed significantly for two weeks or more, a GP or mental health consultation is the right next step. Treatment works, and getting help early makes a significant difference.

Bottom line

None of these symptoms automatically mean something serious is wrong. But they all mean a doctor should hear about them sooner rather than later. Early diagnosis consistently leads to better outcomes — and peace of mind has its own value.
